for the purpose of defining parameter values for GCAM scenarios, figure out the best method for identifying parameter values that are representative of the high and low intervals of demand.

there are several considerations: (0) what price scenario should be used to identify these parameters? (1) demand for Qs, Qn, or Qtot? (2) demand at what income level? (3) demand in what region or aggregate of regions?

these multiple considerations raise the possibility that a single set of parameters may not best represent the high and low intervals under all these conditions. do these parameter values need to be constants or could they be a function of income? or for that matter of prices too? for example, at low income, demand may be more sensitive to prices (and price parameters) than to income (and income parameters); and vice versa at high income.

this would change the demand function, since the function that was estimated on the data had parameters that were constant over the whole dataset, and therefore over all observed income and price levels. so the parameter distributions and associated likelihood values (which are derived from modeled demand) are all based on constant parameters. 

that said, it seems that mechanically at least, there is nothing preventing the use of different parameter values under different conditions. such use would be "consistent" with the existing sample of parameter values in that for the same values of price and income, the two demand functions (constant or non-constant parameters) would give the same level of demand for the same parameters. In other words, demand outcomes are independent from each other, they are not path dependent; demand at a particular income and price level does not depend on what the demand or prices were at another level. As an example, demand when a country reached $10k/cap for a given set of parameters would be the same regardles of whether that country had had demand at $9k/cap modeled with a different set of parameters.

but still, would different parameters under different conditions make sense? probably only if they provided a better fit to the data, and we would only know this if we estimated the parameters separately for different sets of observations that differed in income and possibly prices as well. also, there may not be much of a motivation to do so, because the current functional form and parameters were intended to capture variations in the response of demand to income and to prices at different income and price levels (ie elasticities are not constant).

it is a separate question however -- and perhaps just an academic one -- whether the (constant) ML parameters are consistent with the "central" value of demand at all incomes and prices, for example close to the median or mode. we know the ML parameters are consistent with the mode of the parameter distributions. but are they consistent with the mode of the demand distributions? this is worth checking just for interest. 

	if there is a difference between the two, then it might be worth going further to understand whether we are actually more interested in the mode of demand or in the ML value of demand.

so now back to the question of identifying the best representative high and low demand parameters.

option 1

take the range of demand outcomes for a given region in the neighborhood of 5/95 quantiles, and use a measure of the central tendency of these conditional demand distributions as the parameters for high or low demand. the measure could be their medians, or maybe better the modes. 

a potential (likely?) issue with this approach is that the conditional distributions are relatively broad, which seems to imply that there are still a lot of correlations hidden across these distributions, and central values are not likely to reflect those very well. but it is still worth testing.

one needs to make choices about the considerations listed above: price scenario, income level, food good, and region. for starters, one can take this approach and see how much central tendency parameter values vary over income, across regions, and ultimately for different price scenarios and goods, but at first for Qtot and for default prices.

this yields some differences between HD and LD, although not that much. also differences over income, and a little bit across regions.

note regions differ because different parameter sets may lead to higher or lower demand in a given region, even at the same price and income levels, due to difference in fixed effect (but this is just a constant for Qs only, shouldn't matter)? or maybe due to differences in levels of Qn and Qs at same price/income levels?

option 2

get specific parameter sets (mcmc iterations) consistent with high/low demand intervals under a wide range of conditions. an increasing number of conditions could be applied. for example, for starters, one might target Qtot in the default price scenario, and see which iteration appears in the most high or low demand intervals over all income levels, and as well over all regions.

after that, the test could be extended to additional price scenarios. finally, it could be extended to the two individual goods, rather than the total demand.

results for Pdef scenario:
- HD_Qn: consistent across regions. works well for Qn for rich and poor categories, but not at all for Qs. Also works well for Qtot for the rich category (where Qn is major calorie source). Works hardly at all for middle income. 
- LD_Qn: as in HD, works well for Qn for rich and poor category. However it can also work moderately well for Qs in poor category, and it does not always work well for Qtot in the rich category. Works hardly at all for middle income.
- HD/LD_Qn: This is mostly as expected: when targeting Qn, Qn works well and so does Qtot in conditions where Qn is most of calories: rich category for HD. In LD in poor category, capturing Qn seems to sometimes also capture Qs, and therefore Qtot. Middle income category an exception.

- HD_Qs: consistent across regions. works well for Qs for rich and poor categories, but not for middle income, and not at all for Qn. Also works well for Qtot for the poor category (where Qs is major calorie source). 
- LD_Qs: as in HD, works well for Qs for rich and poor categories, but not for middle income, and not at all for Qn. works well for Qtot in the poor category. some regions it does very poorly for in general (15, 21, 22, etc).
- HD/LD_Qs: This is as expected: when targeting Qs, Qs works well and so does Qtot in conditions where Qs is most of calories: poor category in both HD and LD. Middle income category an exception, as are some regions.

- HD_QsQn: consistent across regions for the middle income category, which does not work well for any food type. for other income categories, it is variable across regions. rich category works well for all three food types in some cases, but sometimes does not capture Qs well. similarly, poor category works well for all three food types in some cases, but sometimes does not capture Qs well.   
- LD_QsQn: mostly consistent across regions. middle income category does not work well. rich category does well in most regions for all three food types, but sometimes does not capture Qs well at all, eg 11, 8, 5. poor category often does reasonably well for Qn and Qtot, less well for Qs (sometimes very poorly for Qs, eg 5, 8, 9, 11, 16, etc.).
- HD/LD_QsQn: This is somewhat surprising, since both Qn and Qs were targeted equally, but it has more trouble capturing Qs, for the poor category as well as sometimes the rich category. But otherwise it does reasonably well overall, capturing Qtot reasonably well in most regions for the poor and rich categories (and Qn, presumably most of the calories). The middle category is an exception.


- HD_Qtot: consistent across regions. works hardly at all for middle income category. in poor category, works well for Qs and Qtot and moderately for Qn. in rich category, works well for Qn and Qtot and not at all for Qs. 
- LD_Qtot: works hardly at all for middle income category. in poor category, sometimes works well for all three food types, but in some regions does not work for Qs at all.  in rich regions, does not work for Qs at all, but does well for Qtot and Qn.
- HD/LD_Qtot: This is mostly as expected: when targeting Qtot, Qtot works well and so does Qs or Qn in conditions where they are most of total calories. So, in the poor category, it also works well for Qs in HD (and to some degree also Qn) and Qn in LD. in the rich category, it also works well for Qn (but not at all for Qs). The middle category is an exception.

- note that strangely the middle income category is not captured well for any good in any of these approaches.

 

option 3

could just accept that different parameter combinations can be the best representative of high or low demand under different conditions, and identify multiple sets. for example, a particular set could be good for HD/LD scenarios for a given price range, or for particular income ranges, or for particular good goods. this could be treated as a backup option if options 1 or 2 do not produce satisfactory results.

simlilarly, it may be the case that even under a particular set of conditions, there are (irreducibly) multiple sets of parameters that could lead to high or low demand. in that case one could simply identify them and pick one at random.
